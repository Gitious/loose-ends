I built an AI agent that handles my inbox.
The hard part wasn't the AI.
==========================================

Subtitle: Why Auth0's Token Vault, FGA, and CIBA are the missing
pieces for trustworthy agents.


It's 11:47 PM on a Tuesday. I have 847 unread emails. A PR has
been stalled for eight days. Someone DM'd me on Slack three hours
ago and I forgot to reply. A calendar conflict is about to explode
into an awkward Slack message tomorrow morning.

This is the problem. Not "too much email." Not "too many tools."
It's that the threads drop, and nobody picks them up.

I spent the last week building an AI agent that does. It's called
Loose Ends. It scans Gmail, Calendar, GitHub, and Slack, finds
the things you forgot about, and — with your permission — handles
them.


THE AI PART WAS EASY. THE TRUST PART WASN'T.
---------------------------------------------

Getting an LLM to read emails and decide what to do is a weekend
project in 2026. Claude Haiku can parse a scheduling request and
draft a reply in under two seconds. That's not novel.

The hard question is: how do you let an agent act on your behalf
across four different services — sending emails, creating calendar
events, posting GitHub comments, messaging on Slack — without
losing control?

This is where it stops being an AI project and starts being an
identity project. And why I built Loose Ends on Auth0.


THREE AUTH0 PRIMITIVES, ONE TRUST STORY
----------------------------------------

1. TOKEN VAULT

Every service integration (Gmail, Calendar, GitHub, Slack) connects
through Auth0's Token Vault. My server never touches your OAuth
tokens. When the agent needs to send an email, it asks Auth0 for
a scoped Gmail token that exists for the duration of that one
API call. If I get breached, your inbox doesn't.

2. FGA (FINE-GRAINED AUTHORIZATION)

Not every permission is created equal. Reading Gmail is different
from sending an email. Adding a calendar event is different from
deleting one. I modeled seven relations across four services:

  - gmail.can_read, gmail.can_reply, gmail.can_delete
  - calendar.can_create, calendar.can_delete
  - github.can_comment, github.can_approve
  - slack.can_send

Every action passes through a live FGA check before the agent can
even attempt it. Users decide exactly what the agent is allowed
to do — per service, per action — and can revoke any of them in
real time.

3. CIBA (GUARDIAN PUSH APPROVAL)

This is the magic. When the agent wants to do something
consequential — send an email, create a meeting, post a comment —
it doesn't just trust a browser session. It fires a push to your
phone with a plain-English description of the exact action:

    "Book 'Coffee with Sarah' Thursday 1pm AND send confirmation reply?"

You tap Approve on your phone. Face ID confirms it's you. Then
and only then does the agent act.

The combination matters. Token Vault limits what's exposed. FGA
limits what's allowed. CIBA confirms what's intended. Remove any
one of them and the whole story falls apart.


THE HERO FLOW: "BOOK & REPLY"
------------------------------

The demo I care most about is this:

You get an email: "Hey, can we grab coffee Thursday at 1pm to talk
Q2 roadmap?"

The old way: context-switch to calendar, check if you're free,
context-switch back, type a reply, hope you didn't double-book.

The new way:

  1. The agent reads the email, extracts:
     "Thursday 1pm, 30 min, coffee with Sarah, topic: Q2 roadmap"
  2. It queries your Google Calendar through a Token Vault-scoped
     token — you're free
  3. It checks FGA — do you allow the agent to book meetings? yes
  4. It drafts a reply in your voice
  5. CIBA push on your phone:
     "Book meeting + send reply?"
  6. You tap approve. Face ID. Done.
  7. The event appears on your calendar. The reply lands in Sarah's
     inbox. In the time it took you to walk from the coffee machine
     to your desk.

Two services. Three Auth0 primitives. One approval. That's the
flow I built the whole app around.


AUTO-ACT: THE AGENT INITIATES
------------------------------

The previous flow is the agent waiting for you to click. The next
level is the agent initiating.

Flip a toggle. Now when your scan finds a dropped thread, the
agent doesn't wait. It sends a CIBA push directly:

  - "Reply to Marcus about design review?"              → Tap
  - "Decline Friday 4pm — conflicts with standup?"      → Tap
  - "Post comment on PR #284 nudging reviewers?"        → Tap
  - "Send Slack DM to Priya: 'responding tomorrow'?"    → Tap

Four dropped threads closed in 90 seconds. From your phone. Without
opening your laptop.

And because every action flows through CIBA, nothing happens
without you. The agent can suggest, initiate, and draft — but it
never executes without your fingerprint.


WHAT I LEARNED BUILDING THIS
-----------------------------

1. "AI agent" is the easy part.
   GPT-4, Claude, whatever — they're all good enough to draft
   emails and reason about calendars. The differentiation isn't
   the model. It's what the model is allowed to touch and what
   happens when it tries.

2. Trust is a product, not a feature.
   Every surface of the app — the permission toggles, the CIBA
   binding messages, the audit log, the "what will the agent do
   and why" reasoning stream — is part of how users decide whether
   to trust the agent. Auth0 gave me the primitives to make
   trust visible.

3. The right abstraction for an LLM is "suggest structured
   actions."
   My early prototype let the agent generate raw API calls. It
   was a disaster. The version that worked: the AI generates
   structured suggestions:

       {
         action: "send_email",
         params: { to, subject, body_hint },
         reason: "..."
       }

   And my code knows how to execute each action type through the
   right FGA-gated, CIBA-protected endpoint.


WHAT'S NEXT
-----------

I want the agent to initiate conversations, not just respond:

  - "Your PM hasn't updated the Q2 doc in a week — nudge them?"
  - "You have three back-to-back meetings Thursday — want me to
    propose a rescheduled standup?"

That's where this is going: an agent that doesn't just react to
your loose ends, but actively maintains the relationships and
commitments you care about.

And none of it works without an identity layer you can actually
trust.
